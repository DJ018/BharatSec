The DPDP Act for small businesses.

A plain-language guide to India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and its 2025 Rules, with a practical checklist.

Does it apply to you?

Almost certainly. The DPDP Act covers digital personal data of people in India: customer names, phone numbers, emails, addresses, employee records, even contact-form submissions. If your business stores any of this, you are a Data Fiduciary under the Act.

Your main duties

  • Notice and consent: tell people clearly what you collect and why, and get their consent (or rely on a listed legitimate use). Let them withdraw consent as easily as they gave it.
  • Security: take reasonable security safeguards, such as encryption, access control, logging and backups.
  • Breach notification: tell affected people and the Data Protection Board of India without delay if personal data is breached.
  • Rights: let people access, correct and erase their data, and offer a way to raise grievances.
  • Retention: delete data once its purpose is served, unless the law requires keeping it.
  • Children: get verifiable parental consent for under-18s, and don't track them or target them with ads.
  • Vendors: you remain responsible for data your processors (hosting, CRM, payroll) handle for you.

Timelines under the 2025 Rules

The DPDP Rules were notified in November 2025 with a phased start. Most business obligations (notice, security safeguards, breach intimation, retention and erasure) apply about 18 months after notification. For a breach, the Rules expect a prompt notice to affected people and to the Board, followed by a detailed report to the Board within 72 hours. Check MeitY's latest notifications for exact dates.

Penalties

  • Up to ₹250 crore for failing to take reasonable security safeguards.
  • Up to ₹200 crore for failing to notify a breach, or breaching duties towards children.
  • Up to ₹50 crore for other breaches of the Act or Rules.

A 10-step starter checklist

  1. List what personal data you collect, where it is stored and who can access it.
  2. Publish a clear privacy notice.
  3. Record consent, and add an easy way to withdraw it.
  4. Name a contact for privacy requests and grievances.
  5. Turn on multi-factor sign-in for email, cloud and admin accounts.
  6. Encrypt data at rest and in transit, and restrict access to those who need it.
  7. Keep access logs and tested backups.
  8. Write a breach plan that covers both DPDP notices and CERT-In's 6-hour report.
  9. Set retention periods and delete old data.
  10. Check that your vendors have contracts covering security and deletion.
